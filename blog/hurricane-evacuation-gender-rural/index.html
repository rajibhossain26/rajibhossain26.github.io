Who gets left behind when a hurricane is coming?

Our new paper in the Journal of Transport Geography looks at how gender and rural or urban location shape hurricane evacuation decisions, and how people would get out, among vulnerable residents of the Florida Panhandle.

In September 2024, Hurricane Helene made landfall in Florida's Big Bend as a Category 4 storm. It became the deadliest Atlantic hurricane since Maria in 2017. Storms like this test the plans that are supposed to get people out of harm's way. Those plans usually assume that people can decide to leave and then drive away. For many residents, neither assumption holds.

In a new paper in the Journal of Transport Geography, my co-authors and I asked two questions about vulnerable residents, including older adults and racial minorities:

  1. How do evacuation decisions differ between women and men, and between urban and rural areas?
  2. How would people get out: their own car, a ride from friends or family, public transport or a ride-hailing service?

The study

We surveyed 318 people across the 18 counties of Northwest Florida's Panhandle, split evenly between urban and rural zip codes: 160 urban and 158 rural, 134 men and 184 women. Rather than lumping everyone together, we analyzed four groups separately: urban men, urban women, rural men and rural women. We used Bayesian logistic regression models that account for income, age, race, housing, health and caregiving.

What we found

1. The gender gap in evacuation is widest in rural areas. Rural men reported the highest evacuation rate (70.8%) and rural women the lowest (58.1%), with urban men (66.7%) and urban women (60.4%) in between. In a simple side-by-side comparison these differences were not statistically significant. Once the models accounted for each group's circumstances, however, rural women stood out: they were less likely to evacuate than both rural men and urban women. This runs against the common finding that women evacuate more readily than men.

2. Different things drive decisions in each group. For rural women, caregiving responsibilities, access to a vehicle, chronic health conditions and access to healthcare were central. For urban men, unstable housing and taking prescribed medication were linked to evacuating, and retirement to staying. Among rural men, being unemployed was linked to a higher likelihood of evacuating. One-size-fits-all messaging misses these differences.

3. Almost everyone plans to drive, but rural residents lean on public transport far more. About 70% of every group would evacuate in their own vehicle. Beyond that, the groups split sharply:

Rural residents were several times more likely than urban women to rely on public transport. That is a concern, because rural transit typically has the fewest routes, the longest gaps between services and the greatest distances to a stop. Urban residents were much more likely to count on a ride from friends or relatives. Ride-hailing barely registered, except among urban women (6.6%).

4. Health, marital status and housing shape how people leave. Access to healthcare, being single, widowed or divorced, and living in vulnerable housing such as mobile homes, nursing homes or shelters were all linked to how people planned to evacuate. Living situation matters as much as who you are.

What it means for emergency planning

  • Plan rural transit as part of evacuation. If rural residents expect a bus or a ride from authorities, that service has to exist, reach them and run early enough.
  • Reach rural women and caregivers directly. People caring for children, older relatives or someone with a health condition face harder trade-offs, so outreach and assistance should be designed around them.
  • Keep healthcare running during evacuations. Medication supplies, medical devices and continuity of care weigh on many people's decisions.
  • Use community networks. Rides from friends and family already carry a large share of urban evacuations, and neighborhood networks can be strengthened before a storm.

Limitations. The survey was online (Pollfish), so people without internet access are under-represented. It asked about past and intended behavior, which may differ from what people do when a storm arrives. The sample over-represents women and adults 65 and older, and subgroup sizes are modest (margin of error about ±7.7% for the urban and rural samples). We see these results as a strong signal for planners and a starting point for larger studies.
